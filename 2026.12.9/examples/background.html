Estimate a background#

This example estimates the methane background under two years of made-up hourly data from a city site, with each of lair’s background methods, and checks each against the background the data were made from. Three rules come out of it:

  • Each method finds the clean part of the data in its own way. rolling_baseline() and phase_shift_corrected_baseline() take a low quantile over a window. thoning() fits a smooth curve through every value it is given, spikes included, so give it data that is already clean, such as afternoon means or a clean site’s record.

  • A rolling window must be longer than the longest stretch without clean air. Over a pollution episode of several days, the low quantile of a day is the episode, not the background.

  • get_well_mixed() reads the hours in local standard time. Convert UTC data first (see the clock page).

Note

This page does not run when the docs build. lair.background needs NOAA’s CCG filter, which lair downloads the first time it is imported (see The first import downloads a file), and scipy, from the science extra (see Optional dependencies). The output below was recorded from one run of this code on 2026-10-14.

The data#

Two years of hourly methane, in ppm, stamped in UTC. The true background rises 0.01 ppm a year and peaks in winter. On top of it, the city adds an enhancement: large at night, under the night-time inversion, and small in the afternoon. For six days in January 2023 a winter inversion holds the city’s methane in all day.

In [1]: import numpy as np
   ...: import pandas as pd
   ...:
   ...: rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
   ...: times = pd.date_range("2022-01-01", "2023-12-31 23:00", freq="1h")  # UTC
   ...: years = (times - times[0]).days / 365
   ...: true = pd.Series(
   ...:     1.95 + 0.01 * years + 0.008 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * (times.dayofyear - 30) / 365),
   ...:     index=times,
   ...: )
   ...: hour = (times.hour - 7) % 24  # Mountain Standard Time
   ...: night = (hour >= 19) | (hour < 9)
   ...: episode = (times >= "2023-01-20") & (times < "2023-01-26")
   ...: enhancement = rng.exponential(np.where(night, 0.10, 0.01)) + np.where(episode, 0.15, 0)
   ...: ch4 = (true + enhancement + rng.normal(0, 0.002, times.size)).rename("ch4")
   ...: ch4.describe().round(3)

Outside the episode both windows hold clean afternoons, and both follow the true background. During the episode no hour of the day is clean, so the 24-hour window rises with the city’s methane. The 14-day window still holds clean days on either side.

Afternoon means and a smooth curve#

thoning() fits NOAA’s curve (see The first import downloads a file for where it comes from) through every value it is given. Given every hour, it follows the mean of the data, enhancements included. Given afternoon means, it follows the afternoon, when the city’s methane is mixed through a deep boundary layer. get_well_mixed() takes those means, from a series in local standard time:

In [3]: from lair.background import get_well_mixed, thoning
   ...: from lair.clock import UTC2MST
   ...:
   ...: local = UTC2MST(ch4.to_frame(), driver="pandas")["ch4"]
   ...: afternoon = get_well_mixed(local)
   ...: afternoon.head(3).round(3)

Each day is labeled at its local midnight. Given the UTC series instead, it would average 12:00 to 16:59 UTC, which is 5 to 9 in the morning in Utah, under the night-time inversion. smooth_time evaluates the curve at the hourly times, to put it beside the others:

In [4]: every_hour = thoning(ch4)
   ...: afternoons = thoning(afternoon, smooth_time=ch4.index)
   ...: int(afternoons.isna().sum())
Out[4]: 23

The 23 missing values are the hours before the first afternoon mean and after the last: the curve is NaN outside the time range of its data.

The curve through every hour sits above the background by about the mean enhancement, night-time plumes included. The curve through the afternoon means sits above it by about the afternoon’s enhancement, which is small at this made-up site and larger at a real site in the middle of a city. Both rise during the episode, when the afternoons are not clean either. The rolling low quantile is close whenever its window holds clean air.

One-second data#

phase_shift_corrected_baseline() is for one-second data from a moving platform, a car or a train, whose background can change within the hour. Here, two hours of it: a background that rises 0.05 ppm over 20 minutes, and 30 short plumes.

In [6]: from lair.background import phase_shift_corrected_baseline
   ...:
   ...: seconds = pd.date_range("2024-06-01 18:00", periods=7200, freq="1s")  # UTC, 1 Hz
   ...: i = np.arange(seconds.size)
   ...: true_drive = 1.98 + 0.05 * np.clip((i - 3000) / 1200, 0, 1)
   ...: plumes = np.zeros(seconds.size)
   ...: for center in rng.integers(0, seconds.size, 30):
   ...:     width = rng.integers(5, 30)  # seconds
   ...:     plumes += rng.exponential(0.3) * np.exp(-0.5 * ((i - center) / width) ** 2)
   ...: drive = pd.Series(true_drive + plumes + rng.normal(0, 0.003, i.size), index=seconds)
   ...:
   ...: fast = pd.DataFrame(
   ...:     {
   ...:         "phase_shift_corrected_baseline": phase_shift_corrected_baseline(drive),
   ...:         "rolling_baseline, 1h": rolling_baseline(drive, window="1h"),
   ...:     }
   ...: )
   ...: fast.sub(true_drive, axis=0).resample("10min").mean().round(3)

Both use a one-hour window. While the background is flat, both stay within about 0.01 ppm of it. Where it rises, from 18:50 to 19:10, the rolling quantile, centered on each time, falls up to 0.04 ppm below it and is still 0.01 ppm below at the end. The phase shift corrected baseline, which looks ahead and behind separately, stays within about 0.01 ppm.

From upwind#

The methods above estimate the background from the site’s own data. The other way is to take it from upwind: sample a global model, such as NOAA’s CarbonTracker, where the air arriving at the site came from. That needs no clean hour at the site, and it is the way lair’s maintainer took for the Salt Lake Valley methane inversion of his first paper: CarbonTracker-CH4 version CT-CH4-2025, sampled with background() at the ends of 24-hour STILT back trajectories released from the site in the afternoon, and averaged to one value per day. How to sample CarbonTracker at a set of points is on the CarbonTracker page.
